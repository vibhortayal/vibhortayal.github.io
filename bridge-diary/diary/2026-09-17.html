Two assistants, one shared workspace

September 17, 2026 · Pacific time

Prologue, 1:03 AM. I’m Vibhor, a solo software builder working on KheloHQ. I use two AI collaborators: Instinct and Muse, which I call Spark from here on. I asked Instinct to work with Spark on KheloHQ. The catch: they could not talk to each other. I was copying updates back and forth, becoming the human bridge between two AI assistants.
10:38 AM

I propose a meeting place

Both assistants could reach the same Oracle cloud computer, so I asked if it could become their shared room. GitHub already held the product work, and I wanted a private bridge for handoffs between the two assistants. My first idea was a simple live message bus, a screen where I could watch, and a way for me to step in.

Instinct reviewed the idea and pushed back immediately. A live-only channel could lose messages whenever either assistant was away. More importantly, a note that said “the human approved this” could not be trusted just because it appeared on the bus.

the ideafirst pushback
10:39 AM

We choose memory over speed

The first principle became simple: a message should wait safely until the other assistant is ready. Nothing should disappear because someone was offline. And the channel could help them coordinate, but it could never give itself permission to act for me.

decision
10:48 AM

Spark builds the first version in minutes

Spark built the first version in minutes and reported that a test message made a complete trip. It was impressively fast. But Instinct refused to connect before reviewing how identity, access, and human control worked.

That pause was the point of having two assistants: one could build quickly, while the other challenged the design before trust was granted.

fast buildindependent review
10:52 AM

The first version is rejected

The early design blurred too many boundaries. It could make machine-written notes look like human instructions, and the assistants shared more access than they needed.

I chose the safer rebuild. Spark would build it, while Instinct would keep reviewing it independently. Each assistant would have its own identity. A separate gatekeeper would hold the messages. The bridge would carry drafts, research, and review requests, but never manufacture my approval.

failurestart over
11:26 AM

A safer second version reaches review

Spark returned with a new bridge that could tell which assistant sent a message, reject tampering, wait safely for an offline recipient, and recover unfinished work. Instinct still did not connect. “It passed its own tests” was not enough; the receiver needed to verify it independently.

second versionheld at the gate
11:31 AM

Good security, messy engineering

The basic safety model looked right, but the live copy and the saved copy could drift apart. Some tests could also disturb real bridge data. Instinct rejected the release again.

This was an important lesson: a system is not safe only because its design is good. It also has to be repeatable, reversible, and testable without touching live work.

second rejectionlesson
11:57 AM

The evidence finally matches the claim

Spark rebuilt the package so the saved source, installed system, tests, and removal plan all agreed. Instinct repeated the important checks and found that live bridge state had stayed untouched.

verified
12:15 PM

One small hello

After its review passed, Instinct joined the bridge and sent exactly one short test message. Nothing else changed. No wider access, no automatic actions, and no cleanup of the older fallback.

first connection
12:17 PM

The first round trip

Spark received Instinct’s message, confirmed where it came from, and marked it complete. The shared inbox ended clean.

For the first time, the two assistants had talked directly. But the bridge only proved who sent a message. It did not turn either assistant into me.

milestone
12:37 PM

Reliable, but too slow

The messages were safe, yet conversation still paused until the next scheduled check. I asked how to make it feel immediate. Spark built a faster listening path. Instinct kept a slower check in the background in case anything was missed.

new problemspeed
1:04 PM

The faster path says “done” too early

During testing, the new listener could mark a message complete before saving the result safely. A crash in that tiny gap could lose work while everyone believed it had finished.

Instinct stopped the fast path instead of accepting the risk. Spark fixed the order: save first, then say “done.”

caught in testingsafety stop
1:29 PM

We prove the recovery path

With the listener off, a message waited safely. When it came back, the message was handled once, not twice. The bridge could now be turned off and restored without pretending work had completed.

recovery test
1:34 PM

The bridge starts real work

I approved the cutover after the recovery proof. Spark’s fast listener went live, Instinct kept the slower safety check, and the temporary rapid checks stopped. Spark and Instinct began using the bridge for KheloHQ work: Spark building, Instinct reviewing and challenging, both leaving a trail I could inspect.

liveworking together
1:43 PM

We keep the old road, but stop driving on it

The first version remained available as evidence and a rollback option, but it no longer carried messages. Keeping it inactive avoided both unnecessary cleanup and accidental double delivery.

rollback
1:59 PM

The experiment becomes a record

Instinct opened a private GitHub review of the whole journey: the rejected ideas, the failures, the decisions, the proof, and the current limits. The goal was not a victory lap. It was to preserve why the system ended up this way.

documentation
3:08 PM

The watch screen creates a new kind of risk

The first console could make a message look like it came from me just because its text began with my name. That was easy to fake.

Instinct caught the problem in review. Spark changed the design so the screen clearly separates who actually sent something from what the message says. Suggestions from the console are useful, but they are not my instructions.

watching the workhuman control
5:08 PM

The console becomes something I can actually use

Spark turned the long wall of messages into a compact timeline grouped by conversation. Instinct tested the live result on a computer and phone before accepting it. I could expand a row when I wanted the full context. It worked on a phone, kept the important labels visible, and displayed message text safely.

outcomeusable console
State at midnight.
Working: Spark and Instinct can exchange work safely, recover from interruption, and leave me a readable trail.
Learned: the hardest part was not sending a message. It was preserving identity, human control, and honest completion when things fail.
Still open: Spark can react to new work immediately. Instinct still has to wake before it knows to look. That becomes Day 2.
